On the wealth cultivation of code farmers

📅 October 29, 2018
📖 1 min read
👁️ 251 views

"The King of Comedy" saw the self-cultivation of actors, and then I wanted to talk about the wealth cultivation of programmers. This is not about financial management, but more about insurance;
What is insurance? It is a mechanism to deal with unknown risks by paying a small amount of insurance premiums, such as car insurance, property insurance, health insurance, etc.;
What insurance do coders need? Equity Sharestock! Overseas is a paradise for many high-tech companies to go public, and then there will be many coders who obtain part of the company's equity through various channels, such as salary, incentives, etc., and can unlock part of the shares every year, and then cash out under heavy taxes.
There are two main risks here, one is the stock price risk and the other is the exchange rate risk;
Because when the equity is granted, it is determined based on the current stock price and exchange rate. For example, ￥1 million, the stock price at that time is $40, and the exchange rate is 6.5. It is granted in 5 years. After conversion, 770 shares are granted every year.
1) If the stock price remains unchanged, you pay 50,000 in taxes every year and get 15,000 in hand. You pay 25,000 in taxes in 5 years and get 75,000 in hand. (Although the tax is heavy, it is acceptable;)
2) If the stock price doubles to $80, you pay 100,000 in taxes every year and receive 300,000. You pay 500,000 in taxes in 5 years and receive 1.5 million in return; (It couldn’t be better;)
3) If the stock price halved to $20, you would pay 20,000 in taxes every year and get 8,000,000 in tax. In 5 years, you would pay 10,000 in taxes and get 40,000 in 40,000. (Although the tax is less, you still can’t accept it. At this time, your biggest risk is to lose 1,000,000, that is, you will lose all when the stock price drops to 0;)

 

Is there any insurance available when stock prices fall? The answer is hedging, also called hedging, which is a tool commonly used by professional players;
When you are granted equity, you are equivalent to holding a long position in the market, but you cannot sell it temporarily, even when watching the stock price fall. At this time, if you buy 8 buy options that will expire in one year, the exercise price is $40 at-the-money put option Put Option, each share is 100 shares. The option price is about 10% of the stock price (mainly time value here). In this example, it is about $4. The total premium you need to pay is $3200, about 20,000. With this put option, let’s look at the above three situations:
1) If the stock price remains unchanged, you pay 50,000 in taxes every year, 20,000 in royalties, and receive 13,000. In five years, you pay 25,000 in taxes, 10,000 in royalties, and receive 65,000; (100,000 less;)
2) If the stock price doubles to $80, you pay 100,000 in taxes every year, 20,000 in royalties, and receive 28,000. You pay 50,000 in taxes in 5 years, 100,000 in royalties, and receive 1,400,000; (100,000 less;)
3) If the stock price halved to $20, you would pay 20,000 in taxes and 20,000 in royalties every year, but the option price has risen to at least $20+. At this time, you can earn at least 80,000+ by selling the options and get 16,000+ in hand. You will pay 10,000 in taxes in 5 years and get 80,000+ in hand;
Do you understand? When the stock price remains unchanged or rises, the put option will expire. In case 2), when the option expires, your long position will also rise, and it is very likely that the increase will exceed your premium loss; but when the stock price falls, you will get more than when the stock price remains unchanged;
The premium is equivalent to your insurance. The guarantee you get is that you can still sell the stock at the exercise price one year later. The worst case scenario is that the stock price remains unchanged. At this time, you will lose all your premium, which is the insurance fee, which is 20,000 per year. This number is your biggest risk, and it is no longer 100% at the beginning;

 

Some people may think that the price of at-the-money put options is relatively high. In this case, you have two options:

1 You can buy out-of-the-money put options, and the price will be much cheaper. For example, a 20% out-of-the-money put option has an exercise price of $32. The option price is about 7% of the stock price, in this case it is $2.8.
1) If the stock price remains unchanged, you pay 50,000 in taxes every year, 1.50,000 in royalties, and receive 13.50,000. You pay 25,000 in taxes in 5 years, 7.50,000 in royalties, and receive 67.50,000; 7.50,000 less;
2) If the stock price doubles to $80, you pay 100,000 in taxes every year, 1.50,000 in royalties, and receive 28.50,000. You pay 50,000 in taxes in 5 years, 7.50 in royalties, and receive 142.50,000; 7.50,000 less;
3) If the stock price halved to $20, you would pay 20,000 in taxes and 1.50,000 in royalties every year, but the option price has risen to at least $12+. At this time, you can earn at least 50,000+ by selling the options and receive 130,000+. You will pay 100,000 in taxes in 5 years and receive 650,000+;
When the stock price remains unchanged or rises, the loss is smaller, and when the stock price falls, the put option also earns less. This depends on personal judgment and preference;

2. You can sell 20% of the call options and 80% of the put options. The 20% premium of the call option can offset the cost of 20% of the put option, that is, you only need to pay 60% of the premium of the put option;

 

One problem here is that you can only lock in profits in advance at the starting price of $40, which means you can only deal with the risk of falling, but cannot adapt to rising prices. For example, the stock price doubles to $80 midway, and then falls back to $40. You only enjoy the joy temporarily, but ultimately lose all the stock price fluctuations and royalties. Is there a more flexible way to protect profits during the rise?
Of course there is, a feasible way is: initially buy $40 at-the-money put options for all positions. When the stock price rises to $50, you sell the $40 put option and buy $50 at-the-money put option; when the stock price rises to $60, you sell the $50 put option and buy $60 at-the-money put option; when the stock price rises to $70, you sell the $60 put option and buy the $70 at-the-money put option...
When the stock price rises above $80, you have an $80 put option in your hand, which can lock in most of the income for you, about 400,000. What you pay is only the premium of 4 put options: $40, $50, $60, $70, which adds up to less than 100,000. The minimum you get is 300,000+, and if you do nothing, your return is 150,000;

 

Finally, we just assumed that the exchange rate remains unchanged. In fact, the exchange rate also has great risks. For example, the exchange rate is 7 when it is granted, and it appreciates midway, and the exchange rate is 6 when it is sold. You will lose 15% of the income, but the method of dealing with it is the same, it is still insurance and hedging!
